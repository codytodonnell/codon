## Overview

Timothy the Turtle is an educational technology that walks young learners through an interactive story that teaches them about the differences between turtles and tortoises. The tool itself is a proof-of-concept to demonstrate how interactive narrative and visualization techniques can be combined to teach challenging scientific information.

## Design Process

Our team of four set out to design a classroom tool that enabled young students to learn difficult concepts independently, with minimal guidance from an instructor. Our goal was to accomplish this using visualization and narrative storytelling. After collecting and analyzing dozens of pieces of existing research around the topics of visualization, storytelling, and in-classroom learning technologies, we found ourselves with a vaguer context than we initially thought we had. We realized that despite our background research and despite the technical goals we had identified, we still were not sure exactly who we wanted to build this kind of tool for and where this kind of tool would be implemented. It was this moment, that we decided that we needed to supplement our background research with data of our own.

## Interviews

To collect more direct information about our learning context, we identified the types of people we wanted to learn from based on the research we had already done and the direction we were hoping to take the project. We decided that teachers were going to be the most accessible, the most relevant, and the most useful to our project goals. At this point we also decided that interviews would be the best way for us to quickly collect the type of information we wanted to know. We interviewed three different teachers from three distinct backgrounds: one former high-school science teacher, one fifth-grade science teacher, and one kindergarten teacher. These interviews yielded several insights into the formal teaching context and helped us identify a more specific problem to frame our work around. The kindergarten teacher we interviewed described a research project she has her students do each year related to the local environment in the state of Maryland. She described how it is difficult for students to conduct research on their own about their chosen animal of interest. She also described that her kindergarteners are all well-adapted to using digital devices. We decided this would be a great context within which to frame our interactive narrative project.

## Iterative Design

Over the course of the design process, we made three iterations of our interactive narrative design. Our first design demonstrated a linear story but fell short of incorporating enough interaction to engage our users and help them reach reach our learning goals. The second design diverged from the linear story structure and attempted to guide learners through a branching build-your-own-adventure type story. This was slightly more engaging but made it challenging for users to come away with the same learned concepts. We ultimately fleshed out a linear story but incorporated several interactive visualizations and animations that guide learners through both the story and the concepts.

## Teacher Evaluation

Now that we had something usable, we wanted to gather feedback to evaluate our design. We built this tool to fit a certain context described by one of the school teachers that we interviewed, so naturally this teacher was a good candidate to evaluate our design. We sent the teacher a direct link to our prototype and gave her several days to play with the tool and generate written feedback. This method was a great way to get valuable remote feedback. The remote test allowed us to respect the teacher’s schedule, allowed her to test the tool without the pressure of an onlooking designer, and helped us both overcome the distance. Her feedback affirmed many of our design decisions and gave us plenty more items to improve for a future iteration: _"Wow that was so creative…I like how you made it a story which is something the kids would definitely like to follow…Is there a button they could click to have it reread?…I think it would be cool if you could click on the turtle at different points and it pulled up a real photograph of the turtle eating food or where it lives…The kids would definitely be able to use it."_

## User Test

The second part of our evaluation was carried out by recording a child using the prototype with the assistance of a parent. Though we were hoping to test the prototype with a kindergartner, we chose to test the tool with a more readily available participant given our team’s time constraints. This participant was a 4 year-old preschool girl from South Florida. We sent a direct link to the prototype to the participant’s mother who agreed to help facilitate the user test. We asked the participant’s mother to present the tool to her daughter on an iPad and video record her interacting with the tool. Because our participant was younger than our intended audience, we asked the mother to read the story aloud and provide minimal guidance if needed. Overall, the participant was able to follow along the story quite well. She keyed into the red buttons very well and understood the clicking interaction on the iPad. The main difficulty she faced was a technical problem where the button required two clicks. Another difficulty we observed was related to the timing of the click interactions. The user consistently wanted to click the buttons as soon as they were present on the screen. At times she would attempt to interact with the button before her mother was finished reading the page’s text. We instructed the mother to ask several questions to the user following the story to test what she learned during the process. To our delight, the user demonstrated a nearly perfect retention of the concepts presented in the story.
